# Drafting Communications Where Power Dynamics Matter

For emails, Slack messages, and talking points where hierarchy, politics, or relationship stakes shape what works: pushing back upward, asking for resources, delivering bad news, declining a senior request, raising a concern about a peer, announcing something contested.

## Before drafting

Establish (from context or one quick question):
1. **Relative position** — writing up, down, or across? How much standing does the user have with this reader?
2. **The reader's stake** — what does this message threaten or offer them? (Check currencies: time, status, control, risk exposure.)
3. **The real goal** — the decision, the relationship, the paper trail, or some mix. The mix determines the draft.
4. **Channel** — anything emotionally loaded or status-threatening usually belongs in conversation, with the written message as setup or follow-up. Say so when the user is about to send something that should be said.

## Structural principles

**Writing upward:**
- Lead with the headline; senior readers triage. Context after the ask, not before.
- One ask per message. Multiple asks get the easiest one answered and the rest ignored.
- Frame in the reader's currency: their goals, their risks, their commitments — not the user's effort or feelings.
- Strip hedges ("just checking," "sorry to bother," "this might be off-base") unless softening is the deliberate strategy. Replace apology with appreciation: not "sorry for the delay," but "thanks for your patience."
- Make the yes easy: propose a default ("I'll proceed with X Friday unless you'd rather discuss") rather than an open question, when the user has standing to do so.

**Pushing back / disagreeing upward:**
- Affirm the shared goal first, genuinely and in one line.
- Disagree with the plan, never the person's judgment. "I see a risk in the timeline" not "I think you've underestimated this."
- Bring data or a concrete scenario, not adjectives.
- Offer an alternative or a question, never a bare objection: a bare "this won't work" forces the senior person to defend; "what would have to be true for this timeline to hold?" lets them discover the problem.
- Commit visibly once the decision is made, whichever way it goes — that's what makes the next disagreement hearable.

**Delivering bad news upward:**
- Never let them hear it elsewhere first; speed beats polish.
- Structure: what happened → impact → what's being done → what's needed from them. No burying, no blame-spreading.
- Pair every problem with an owner and a next step, even a provisional one.

**Asking for resources / headcount / budget:**
- Frame as an investment with a return in the reader's currency, not a need of the user's team.
- Anticipate the obvious objection in the message ("Cheaper option B exists; here's why it costs more within two quarters").
- Name the cost of inaction — scarcity and risk move decisions more than upside.

**Across (peers, other teams):**
- No authority to lean on; everything is exchange. Open with what's in it for them or what you're offering, not what you need.
- Reciprocity language works when honest: acknowledge past help, offer future help.
- Public channels create commitment pressure but also status risk for the reader — request publicly only when the relationship is already cooperative.

**Downward:**
- Power amplifies every word; mild concern reads as criticism, a question reads as a directive. Calibrate down.
- Be explicit about what's a decision vs. a suggestion vs. an open question — ambiguity from above creates anxious guessing below.

## Output format

Produce the draft, then one or two lines on the strategic choice it embodies ("This version leads with the risk to his launch date because that's what he's accountable for this quarter").

When there's a genuine strategic fork — not just tone, but different outcomes — offer labeled variants: e.g. "Hold the deadline" vs. "Offer the trade," or "Soft flag" vs. "Formal escalation." Two variants usually; three only when the forks are real. Note what each prioritizes and risks.

If a message-composition tool is available in the environment, use it for the final drafts.

## Red lines

Don't draft messages designed to deceive, create false paper trails, threaten, or engineer someone's humiliation. Offer the legitimate version of the goal instead: a clean escalation, a firm boundary, an honest record of events.
