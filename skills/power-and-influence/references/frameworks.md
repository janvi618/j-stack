# Frameworks: How Power and Influence Work

The shared vocabulary for this skill. All concepts here are standard, widely-taught organizational behavior — use them to diagnose, then translate into plain language for the user.

## 1. Bases of power (French & Raven)

Where does someone's power come from? Most people have more bases available than they use.

- **Legitimate** — formal authority from role or title. Fast but shallow; produces compliance, not commitment, and evaporates when the title does.
- **Reward** — ability to give things people want (raises, plum assignments, visibility, praise).
- **Coercive** — ability to punish or withhold. Effective short-term, corrosive long-term; breeds resentment and information-hiding.
- **Expert** — credibility from knowledge or skill. Durable, but domain-bound and requires staying visibly current.
- **Referent** — people follow you because they like, trust, or identify with you. The slowest to build and the most durable.
- **Informational** — control over information or access to it (sometimes folded into expert power). Includes network position: being the person who connects otherwise-disconnected groups.

**Diagnostic use:** Map both parties. Influence problems are often a mismatch — e.g., a user leaning on expert power with an executive who responds to referent and legitimate power. The fix is usually borrowing a base they lack (get a sponsor, build the relationship) rather than doubling down on the one they have.

## 2. Influence principles (Cialdini)

Why people say yes, mostly automatically:

- **Reciprocity** — people repay favors, concessions, and information. Give first.
- **Commitment & consistency** — people act in line with prior public commitments. Get small agreements early.
- **Social proof** — people look to what similar others are doing. "Two other teams have already adopted this."
- **Liking** — similarity, genuine compliments, cooperation toward shared goals.
- **Authority** — credentials, expertise signals, third-party endorsement.
- **Scarcity** — limited windows and unique opportunities motivate. Use honestly; manufactured scarcity destroys trust when discovered.
- **Unity** — shared identity ("we" groups) amplifies everything above.

**Ethical line:** these work as descriptions of how decisions happen. Using them to align genuine interests is influence; using them to get someone to act against their interests is manipulation. Coach toward the former.

## 3. Currencies of exchange (Cohen & Bradford's "influence without authority" tradition)

Influence with peers and skip-levels is trade. People value different currencies:

- **Task currencies** — resources, help, information, faster turnaround.
- **Position currencies** — visibility, recognition, association with winners, reputation protection.
- **Relationship currencies** — acceptance, understanding, personal support.
- **Personal currencies** — gratitude, ownership, feeling ethical/competent.
- **Inspiration currencies** — meaning, excellence, contribution to a larger goal.

**Diagnostic use:** "What does this person actually want that I can give?" When someone is uncooperative, list what currencies the user holds; there's almost always more inventory than they think.

## 4. Stakeholder mapping

For any decision with multiple players, place each stakeholder on two axes:

- **Power** over the outcome (high/low)
- **Interest/stance** toward your position (supporter / neutral / opponent)

Strategy by quadrant: high-power supporters → arm them to advocate; high-power opponents → engage early and privately, find what they'd need; high-power neutrals → these usually decide the outcome, invest here; low-power players → keep informed, don't over-invest, but note that today's low-power player is tomorrow's decision-maker.

Also identify the **decision-maker vs. the influencers vs. the veto-holders** — they're often different people, and pitching the formal decision-maker while ignoring the trusted advisor who has their ear is a classic failure.

## 5. Status and threat

Much resistance that looks substantive is actually status protection. People react badly when a proposal implies they were wrong, reduces their turf, or makes someone else look better. Signals: objections that shift when answered, opposition disproportionate to stakes, "process" complaints.

Countermeasures: give credit generously, let the resister improve the idea so they own part of it, frame the proposal as extending (not correcting) their prior work, deliver challenges privately.

## 6. Power dynamics in conversation

- **Asking vs. telling:** questions can carry authority ("What would have to be true for this to work?") while preserving the other person's status.
- **Hedging:** qualifiers ("just," "sort of," "this might be a dumb idea") lower perceived confidence. Strip them in upward communication unless deliberately softening.
- **Air time and interruption patterns** signal and create status. Coach users to claim space by preparing one crisp, early contribution rather than fighting for volume.
- **Warmth and competence** are the two judgments people form fastest. Influence requires both; competence without warmth reads as threat, warmth without competence reads as weakness. Most professionals over-index on proving competence.

## 7. Building durable power (career-level)

- **Deliver scarce value visibly.** Power follows dependence: control something the organization needs and can't easily replace.
- **Network into structural holes.** Be the bridge between groups that don't otherwise talk. Brokers learn first and get credited as originators.
- **Cultivate sponsors, not just mentors.** Mentors advise; sponsors spend their capital on your behalf in rooms you're not in. Sponsors are earned by being a safe bet — perform, be loyal, make them look good.
- **Manage visibility deliberately.** Quality work unseen is, politically, work that didn't happen. Routine, non-bragging visibility: brief updates upward, credited shared wins, teaching what you know.
- **Power is perishable.** Bases erode (expertise dates, networks decay, sponsors leave). Audit and reinvest periodically.

## 8. The ethics frame to coach from

Three questions distinguish influence from manipulation:
1. Would this approach survive the other person finding out exactly what you did and why?
2. Are you helping them act in their genuine interest, or against it?
3. Are you building a reputation you can live with?

If any answer is no, find another path to the same goal. There almost always is one, and it's usually more durable.
