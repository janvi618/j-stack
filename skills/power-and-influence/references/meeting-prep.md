# Preparing for High-Stakes Meetings and Negotiations

For prep before the room: pitching a proposal, a decision meeting with multiple stakeholders, a performance/comp conversation, a negotiation with a vendor or another team, a skip-level, a board or exec readout.

## Prep sequence

### 1. Define the win, the acceptable, and the walk-away
- **Win:** the best realistic outcome.
- **Acceptable:** the minimum the user would take and still call it progress.
- **Walk-away / fallback (BATNA):** what happens if there's no agreement — and how to strengthen it *before* the meeting. The party with the better fallback holds the leverage; sometimes the most valuable prep is improving the fallback (another offer, an alternative vendor, a plan B for the project), not rehearsing arguments.
- Also define the **relationship outcome**: some meetings are worth losing on substance to win on standing, and vice versa. Make the user choose consciously.

### 2. Map the room
For each person who matters:
- Power over this outcome (decide / influence / veto / execute)
- Current stance (for / against / undecided) and *why* — in their currency, not the user's
- What would move them, and who they listen to

Flag the two classic traps:
- Pitching the formal decision-maker while ignoring the advisor who has their ear.
- Ignoring the quiet loser — whoever loses turf, budget, or status if this succeeds is the likely source of resistance, voiced through proxies and "process concerns."

### 3. Pre-wire the decision
The meeting should confirm a decision, not discover one. Coach the user to:
- Meet key stakeholders 1:1 beforehand — supporters get armed with talking points, opponents get heard privately (objections shrink without an audience), the undecided get the pitch tailored to their currency.
- Let influential skeptics improve the proposal so they enter the room as part-owners.
- Know the count before walking in. If the votes aren't there, the right move is usually to delay the meeting, not to gamble on eloquence.

### 4. Build the narrative
- One sentence: what is being decided and why now. If the user can't say it in one sentence, the meeting will wander.
- Lead with the conclusion for senior audiences; build to it only for hostile-but-junior ones.
- Frame in the audience's dominant currency (risk reduction, growth, cost, reputation — pick the one the key decider actually cares about).
- Prepare the **objection map**: the three most likely objections, each with a response and supporting evidence. Distinguish substantive objections (answer with data) from status objections (answer with credit, inclusion, and private follow-up — more data makes status objections worse).

### 5. Plan the in-room dynamics
- Where to make the first contribution: early and crisp claims standing for the whole meeting.
- What to concede gracefully — pre-pick the giveable points so concessions look generous rather than forced.
- Who might hijack, and the redirect line ("Worth a real discussion — can I take that with you after, so we can close on the decision today?").
- The closing move: never end without explicit next steps, owners, and a date. Decisions that aren't summarized out loud get re-litigated.

### 6. Rehearse the hardest 90 seconds
Offer to role-play: the opening pitch, the toughest objection, or the moment the senior skeptic pushes back. Play the skeptic realistically — pressure-test, don't flatter.

## Negotiation-specific additions

- **Interests behind positions:** the stated demand ("20% discount," "two more engineers") always serves an underlying interest (budget pressure, deadline fear, status). Trade across interests — give what's cheap to you and valuable to them.
- **Anchor deliberately:** the first credible number frames the range. If the user has good information, anchoring favorably helps; if they're information-poor, let the other side reveal first.
- **Bundle, don't serialize:** negotiate issues as a package so trades are possible ("flexible on timeline if the scope holds"), not one-by-one where each becomes a win/lose fight.
- **Concession pattern signals:** decreasing concession sizes signal a genuine limit; erratic ones invite further pushing.
- **Silence and the flinch are tools** — after stating a number, stop talking.
- **Never bluff a walk-away** the user can't execute; called bluffs destroy standing permanently.

## Output format

End every prep with a short "what actually matters" close: the 2–3 moves that will most determine the outcome, stated plainly. Long checklists comfort; priorities win.
