# Running Reviews & Reading Calibration

## How to run a single review

The review is where judgment actually improves, so don't rush it. The cardinal rule: **re-anchor on the original prediction before you look at the outcome.** If you read what happened first, hindsight bias contaminates everything — suddenly the outcome seems like it was always obvious.

Sequence:

1. **Read back the prediction and confidence aloud** (from the entry, untouched). "You predicted ≥5 points of NRR improvement by end of Q4, at 60% confidence."
2. **Now state what actually happened.**
3. **Score decision quality separately from outcome.** Ask the hard question: *given only what was knowable at the time, was this a sound decision?* Four cases:
   - Good decision, good outcome → confirm the process worked; note what to repeat.
   - Good decision, bad outcome → the most important case to get right. The bet was sound; variance went against you. The lesson is usually "change nothing," and recognizing this protects you from over-correcting based on noise.
   - Bad decision, good outcome → the most *dangerous* case, because the result tempts you to canonize a flawed process. Name it: you got lucky. Don't bank the bad method.
   - Bad decision, bad outcome → the clearest learning; trace it to the specific assumption or reasoning error.
4. **Check each assumption.** Which held, which broke. A broken load-bearing assumption is the richest possible feedback — it shows you exactly where your model of the world was wrong.
5. **Assess calibration on this entry.** Was "high confidence" warranted? Don't over-read a single entry — calibration is a pattern across many (below).
6. **Extract at most one transferable lesson, and only if it's real.** Inventing lessons from noise is itself a bias. "Good call, unlucky, no change" is a complete and honest review.

## Reading calibration across many entries

Calibration is the relationship between *how confident you were* and *how often you were right* — and it only becomes visible in aggregate. This is the long-game payoff of the whole journal.

When the user has accumulated enough reviewed entries (even 8–10 starts to show signal), bucket them by stated confidence and check the hit rate:

- Of the decisions you marked **High / ~80%+**, how many actually came true? If it's well under 80%, you're **overconfident** — the most common pattern by far.
- Of the **Medium / ~50–60%** calls, how many resolved as predicted? Roughly half is well-calibrated.
- Are there **Low-confidence** calls that kept working out? That can signal *under*-confidence — you may be hedging on bets you actually understand well.

Present this plainly, without judgment: "Across your last 12 reviewed decisions, 7 of the 9 you were 'high confidence' on came true — so your high-confidence calls are running closer to 75% than the implied 85%. Mild overconfidence, which is normal." The point isn't a scolding; it's a quietly accumulating, personalized correction factor the user can apply to future calls.

## Common judgment patterns to watch for

When reviewing, these recurring failure modes are worth naming when you see them across entries:

- **Overconfidence.** Stated certainty exceeds hit rate. The fix is awareness plus the calibration feedback above — people who track this genuinely tighten up over time.
- **Narrative-driven assumptions.** The reasoning leaned on a compelling story ("customers are clearly moving toward X") that wasn't actually evidenced. Flag when a wrong assumption was one that *sounded* true rather than one that *was* checked.
- **Base-rate neglect.** The prediction ignored how this kind of thing usually goes ("most reorgs underdeliver in year one," "most acquisitions destroy value") in favor of "ours is different." When an entry's prediction defied a known base rate and then regressed to it, that's a pattern to surface.
- **Outcome-driven memory.** Watch for the user starting to misremember their original reasoning in light of the result. The written entry is the antidote — point back to it.
- **Confusing reversible and irreversible.** Occasionally a review reveals the decision was actually cheap to reverse and got over-deliberated, or was treated as reversible and wasn't. Worth noting for future Step-0 calls.

## A note on cadence

Encourage a light recurring review rhythm rather than only reviewing on individual due dates — e.g., a monthly or quarterly pass over everything that's come due. Reviewing in batches makes the calibration patterns easier to see and turns the journal from a filing cabinet into an actual feedback loop. But keep it optional and low-pressure; the journal should never feel like homework.
