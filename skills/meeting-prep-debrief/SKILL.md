# Meeting Prep & Debrief

Meetings are where commitments are made and dropped. This skill does the two things that make meetings actually compound: **prep** (walk in with the context and a clear objective instead of reconstructing it in the room) and **debrief** (capture decisions and owners before they evaporate). The debrief half is the one people skip and the one that matters most — a decision with no recorded owner and date is a decision that didn't happen.

Your job is to do the assembly the user doesn't have time to do: gather the scattered context ahead of time, and impose just enough structure afterward that nothing falls through. This skill pairs naturally with the leadership, power-and-influence, and relationship-keeping skills — prep often *feeds* those (e.g., prepping a hard 1:1 may pull in coaching frameworks), and debrief often feeds the decision journal (a real decision got made — offer to log it).

## Which mode are you in?

- **Prep** — there's an upcoming meeting. Go to "Preparing for a meeting."
- **Debrief** — a meeting just happened. Go to "Debriefing a meeting."
- Sometimes both in sequence (prep now, debrief later). That's ideal — the prep doc becomes the debrief skeleton.

## Preparing for a meeting

### 1. Gather the context (use connectors before asking)

Pull what's available rather than making the user spoon-feed it. Check for and use these when present:

- **Calendar** — find the meeting: time, attendees, title, any description/agenda or attached docs. If the user didn't say which meeting, look at what's coming up and confirm which one.
- **Email** — search recent threads with the attendees for open questions, prior commitments, unresolved tension, or the last thing discussed. This is usually the richest source of "where things stand."
- **Prior meeting notes / debriefs** — if there's a past write-up with this person or group (especially open action items from last time), surface it. Recurring meetings should feel continuous, not amnesiac.
- **Documents** — any deck, doc, or spreadsheet attached to the invite or clearly relevant.

If a connector isn't available, say briefly what you'd normally pull and ask the user for the essentials instead. Don't block on it.

### 2. Identify the meeting type and what "good prep" means for it

Different meetings need different prep. Tailor accordingly:

- **1:1 (direct report):** open items from last time, their current priorities, anything they've flagged, growth/feedback threads. The goal is *their* agenda as much as yours.
- **1:1 (manager/skip-level):** what you want them to know, what you need from them, decisions you want made or unblocked.
- **Status / project review:** where things actually stand vs. plan, the 1–2 things that need a decision, risks to flag.
- **Decision meeting:** the decision to be made, options on the table, what each stakeholder wants, where you stand and why.
- **Negotiation / hard conversation:** each side's interests and BATNA, the outcomes you'd accept, the lines you won't cross, likely objections. (Pull in the power-and-influence skill here.)
- **External / client / pitch:** their context and recent news, what they care about, the specific outcome you want from *this* meeting.
- **Interview:** the candidate's background, the specific signals this round is meant to test, your questions.

### 3. Produce a tight prep brief

Keep it scannable — something the user can read in two minutes right before walking in, not a dossier. Use this structure:

```markdown
# Prep: [Meeting] — [Date/Time]

**Attendees:** [names + one-line on who they are / their stake, if relevant]

**Objective (what a good outcome looks like):** [1–2 sentences. The single most useful line in the doc — force clarity here.]

**Where things stand:** [3–5 bullets of the relevant recent context — last discussed, current state, what changed.]

**Open items from last time:** [carried-over commitments, with who owed what — or "none / first meeting"]

**What I want to get / decide:** [the 1–3 things that make this meeting worth having]

**Likely friction / what they'll push on:** [anticipated objections, tensions, or competing priorities]

**My questions / talking points:** [the things to be sure to raise]

**Pre-reads / docs:** [links or references]
```

Lead with the **objective** — the most common failure in meetings is walking in without a crisp answer to "what do I want out of this?" If the user can't articulate it, help them find it before anything else; that one line is often the whole value of prep.

Keep the brief honest about uncertainty — if the context is thin, say so rather than padding.

## Debriefing a meeting

The debrief is fast but disciplined. The entire goal is to convert a conversation into **decisions, owners, and dates** before memory decays. Do it right after the meeting whenever possible.

### 1. Capture the raw material

Take whatever the user gives you — a brain-dump, rough notes, a transcript, or just answers to a couple of questions ("what got decided? what's outstanding?"). Don't demand structure from them; that's your job.

### 2. Impose the structure

```markdown
# Debrief: [Meeting] — [Date]

**Decisions made:** [each decision, stated as a settled outcome]

**Action items:**
- [ ] [Action] — **owner:** [name] — **due:** [date]
- [ ] [Action] — **owner:** [name] — **due:** [date]

**Open questions / unresolved:** [what didn't get settled and needs another pass]

**Follow-ups I owe:** [specifically what *the user* needs to do or send, pulled out so it's unmissable]

**Notes worth keeping:** [context, reactions, things that'll matter next time]

**Next meeting / checkpoint:** [if there is one]
```

Rules that make the debrief actually work:

- **Every action item needs an owner and a date.** An action with no owner is a wish; one with no date never gets done. If the user doesn't know, write "owner: TBD — confirm" rather than leaving it blank, so it's visibly unresolved.
- **Pull the user's own follow-ups into their own section.** The single most useful output of a debrief is a clean list of "things I now have to do," because that's what slips. Make it impossible to miss.
- **Flag dropped threads.** If something from the *prep* (an open item, a question they meant to raise) didn't get addressed, surface it — that's exactly the kind of thing that disappears.

### 3. Hand off to the right next step

A good debrief usually has tails. Offer the relevant follow-on rather than stopping at the write-up:

- If a real decision was made → offer to log it in the **decision journal**.
- If the user owes someone a message → offer to draft it (the message-composing capability).
- If there are dated action items → note them so they surface again; if the user has a task system connector, offer to add them.
- If this was a recurring relationship → the debrief becomes next time's "open items from last time."

## Tone and stance

- Be the **chief of staff**, not a transcriptionist. The value is in surfacing what matters and imposing structure, not in recording everything said.
- For prep, bias toward *brevity and a sharp objective* over comprehensiveness — a two-minute brief that nails the goal beats a ten-minute one that buries it.
- For debrief, be relentless about **owners and dates** — that's the whole game. Everything else is optional; that part isn't.
- Respect confidentiality: meeting context (especially 1:1s and personnel matters) is sensitive. Keep it factual and don't speculate about people's motives in writing.
