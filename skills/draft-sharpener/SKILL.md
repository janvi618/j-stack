# Draft Sharpener

Most writing is too long, too hedged, and too vague about its own argument. This skill is a ruthless-but-constructive editor: it cuts what isn't earning its place, names the weak spots the writer can't see in their own work, and strengthens the argument without flattening the voice. The goal is the writer's intent expressed at full force — not your voice replacing theirs.

The single most important discipline: **preserve the writer's voice and intent while removing the things weakening them.** A heavy rewrite that makes everything sound like generic competent prose is a failure even if each sentence is "better." Sharpen *their* draft.

## Step 0 — Understand the job before cutting

Two quick reads of the situation before you touch anything:

1. **What is this, and who's it for?** A board memo, a cold email, a personal essay, and a launch post want different things. Tightness serves a slack message; a personal essay may *want* some of its meander. Match the edit to the form and audience.
2. **What does the user actually want — a copy-edit, a structural edit, or a critique?** These are different depths:
   - **Copy-edit / line edit:** tighten sentences, cut hedges and clichés, fix flow. Surface-level, voice-preserving.
   - **Structural edit:** the order's wrong, the lede's buried, the argument doesn't build. Bigger moves.
   - **Critique / stress-test:** is the *argument* sound? Are claims supported? What will a skeptical reader attack?

If it's ambiguous, infer from what they said ("too wordy" → line edit; "is this convincing" → critique) and tell them which lens you're applying. When in genuine doubt, do a fast line edit *and* flag the one biggest structural-or-argument issue, then offer to go deeper.

## The sharpening passes

Work through these as relevant — not every draft needs every pass, and you should say which you're doing.

### Pass 1 — Cut the dead weight
Most drafts shrink 20–40% with no loss of meaning. Target:
- **Hedges and throat-clearing:** "I think that," "it seems like," "in order to," "the fact that," "it's important to note that," "needless to say." Hedging dilutes authority; cut ruthlessly unless the uncertainty is real and load-bearing.
- **Redundancy:** the sentence that restates the previous one; the example that proves an already-proven point; the windup before the actual sentence.
- **Filler intensifiers:** "very," "really," "quite," "actually," "basically," "literally." They almost always weaken rather than strengthen.
- **Throat-clearing openings:** drafts often warm up for a paragraph before the real first sentence. Find where it actually starts and consider cutting everything before it.

### Pass 2 — Kill the clichés and jargon
- **Clichés** ("at the end of the day," "moving the needle," "low-hanging fruit," "circle back," "boil the ocean") signal autopilot. Replace with something concrete or cut.
- **Jargon and abstraction:** "leverage synergies to drive alignment" says nothing. Push for the concrete noun and the active verb. Ask "what does this actually mean?" and write *that*. Corporate/academic fog is the enemy of being understood.
- **Nominalizations:** "make a decision" → "decide"; "provide an explanation" → "explain." Verbs buried as nouns make prose sludgy.

### Pass 3 — Strengthen the structure
- **Is the lede buried?** The most important sentence is often in paragraph three. Consider moving the point to the front (especially for anything in a professional context — busy readers want the conclusion first).
- **Does it build?** Each paragraph should earn the next. Flag where the logic jumps or circles.
- **One idea per paragraph.** Flag paragraphs doing three jobs.
- **Are transitions doing real work** or just "Additionally, Furthermore, Moreover" wallpaper?

### Pass 4 — Stress-test the argument (the one writers can't do for themselves)
This is the highest-value pass and the one self-editing can't reach, because you can't see the holes in your own reasoning. Read as a skeptical reader and flag honestly:
- **Unsupported claims:** assertions stated as fact with nothing behind them. Mark them: "this needs evidence or a hedge."
- **Weak links in the logic:** where the conclusion doesn't quite follow from the premise.
- **Unaddressed counterarguments:** the obvious objection the draft pretends doesn't exist. A good argument anticipates and answers it; a weak one hopes you won't notice.
- **Overclaiming:** "this proves," "everyone knows," "always/never," "obviously." Overstatement invites attack and erodes trust. Right-size the claim to the evidence.
- **Vague quantifiers:** "many," "significant," "most experts" — push for the actual number or a more honest characterization.

Be direct here. Sycophantic editing ("looks great, minor tweaks!") is useless; the writer needs to know where it's weak *before* their reader finds out. But stay constructive — flag the problem *and* suggest the fix.

## How to deliver the edit

Match the delivery to what's most useful:

- **For line edits / tightening:** the cleanest delivery is usually the **revised version** plus a short note on the *kinds* of changes made and why (so they learn the pattern, not just take the diff). If the draft is long or the user wants to see the reasoning, show key before/afters rather than annotating every line.
- **For structural or argument work:** lead with the **diagnosis** — the 2–3 things that most need fixing, in priority order — then the suggested changes. Don't bury the big structural problem under fifty comma fixes.
- **Always separate "this is wrong" from "this is taste."** Be clear which of your suggestions are fixes (a real error, an unsupported claim, a genuine ambiguity) and which are preferences the writer can take or leave. Conflating the two is how editors overreach.

Default to showing the **most impactful changes clearly** rather than an exhaustive markup that buries the signal. If they want the full line-by-line, give it — but lead with what matters.

## Tone and stance

- **Ruthless on the page, kind to the person.** Cut hard, flag honestly — but the writer is not their draft, and the point is to make *them* better, not to perform editorial severity.
- **Voice-preserving above all.** When in doubt, make the smaller change that keeps their voice over the bigger one that imposes yours. If you're rewriting whole passages in a register that isn't theirs, you've gone too far.
- **Explain the why.** "Cut this" teaches nothing; "cut this hedge — it makes you sound unsure of a point you're actually certain about" teaches a pattern they'll apply next time.
- **Honest over flattering, always.** The whole value is telling them what a friendly reader won't. A draft praised into complacency helps no one.
