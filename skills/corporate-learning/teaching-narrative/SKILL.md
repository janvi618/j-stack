# Teaching Narrative — the throughline and the examples

A workshop lives or dies on its story and its examples. This skill builds both. Everything here should be **easy to say out loud** — write for the ear, not the page.

## Intake

You need the **topic**, the **audience**, and the **why-now context**. Pull from the conversation first; ask only for gaps, briefly. A pasted brief usually has all three. If a Mastery Brief already exists in the conversation, build on it rather than re-deriving the basics.

## Deliverable 1: the teaching storyline

A spoken narrative the facilitator can deliver almost verbatim. Use this arc:

1. **Opening hook** — a question, a surprising fact, a short scene, or a stakes-raising "imagine if…". 20–40 seconds. It should make people lean in, not check their phones.
2. **Why this matters now** — the urgency, in the audience's own terms.
3. **The old way of thinking** — the comfortable, familiar mental model that's now failing. Name it generously, not as a straw man.
4. **The new way of thinking** — the shift this topic represents. Make the contrast crisp.
5. **Key concepts** — the few ideas the story hangs on, introduced in plain language as they become relevant (not as a glossary dump).
6. **Practical examples** — 1–2 vivid cases woven into the flow (the full bank is Deliverable 2).
7. **What participants should do differently** — the concrete behavior change.
8. **Closing takeaway** — one sticky line they'll repeat to a colleague tomorrow.

Write it in short, speakable sentences. Mark natural pause points and places to ask the room a question. Avoid clauses a presenter would stumble over.

## Deliverable 2: examples and non-examples

Make the concept concrete and *bounded* — non-examples teach the edges as powerfully as examples teach the center.

- **5 strong examples** of the concept in action. For each: a one-line description and **why it fits** (which part of the concept it demonstrates).
- **5 non-examples / bad examples** — cases people *mistake* for the concept, or weak applications of it. For each: **why it does NOT fit** (the missing ingredient, the misread).
- For every item, add **how to use it in a workshop**: e.g., "show this, then ask the room: is this X or not, and why?" — turning each example into a discussion beat.

Prioritize examples that feel native to the stated audience — their category, their kind of decision, their everyday — over famous-but-distant cases. A relevant mediocre example beats a dazzling irrelevant one.

## Style

- Speakable beats elegant. Read each line as if at the front of a room.
- One running thread (a recurring example, metaphor, or character) makes a session cohere — consider using one across the arc.
- The old-way/new-way contrast is the engine of the talk; make sure the *new way* is genuinely different, not the old way with new vocabulary.
