# The Seven Conditions of High-Performing Teams

This is the core diagnostic lens. Almost every team problem is a weakness in one or two of these. Use it to name the *actual* failure mode before prescribing anything — people chronically mislabel a coordination problem as a people problem.

For each condition: what it looks like when present, the symptoms of its absence, and the leader's move to build it.

---

## 1. Clear direction and hard priorities

**Present:** The team can give a brutally clear answer to three questions — *what matters most now, what trade-offs we will make, and what success looks like.* There is one primary outcome, two or three supporting priorities, and explicit "not now" choices.

**Absent (symptoms):** Too many priorities carried at once; activity confused with progress; trade-offs postponed until conflict becomes political; everyone is "aligned" on vague themes but no one can name the single most important outcome the same way.

**Leader's move:** Name *one* primary outcome, two or three supporting priorities, and the things you are explicitly *not* doing this quarter. Make trade-offs explicit *before* the crisis forces them. The research backing: specific, difficult goals beat vague goals for group performance; mission-command doctrine rests on shared understanding of intent.

---

## 2. Role clarity and decision rights

**Present:** Every critical workstream has a named owner. The team distinguishes consultation, recommendation, approval, and execution. When responsibility shifts, the handoff is explicit.

**Absent (symptoms):** Duplicated effort and gaps simultaneously; endless "shadow consultation" because no one knows who actually decides; "I thought you had it"; decisions that stall because the decider was never named.

**Leader's move:** For each major topic, name the **Owner** (accountable for movement/outcome), the **Decision-maker** (makes the call after input), the **Core inputs** (must be consulted), the **Execution leads**, and the **Escalation trigger**. The operating rule: *if everyone is accountable, no one is accountable.* (Full template in `operating-system.md`.) Backing: team science on role differentiation, interdependence, and transactive memory (knowing who knows what and who owns what); aviation and healthcare make authority/responsibility transfers explicit.

---

## 3. Shared mental models and situation awareness

**Present:** The team gets on the same page fast. Everyone shares an understanding of the task, the environment, and who knows what — before execution, not during conflict.

**Absent (symptoms):** Each function stays trapped in its local view — marketing optimizes message, sales optimizes the retailer ask, supply chain optimizes feasibility, finance optimizes margin — and *nobody is optimizing the enterprise outcome.* Problems are "discovered sequentially in late-stage conflict."

**Leader's move:** Build shared models *before* the work through pre-briefs, decision memos, visual operating plans, and explicit scenario discussion. For interdependent work, get the consumer insight, the customer/retailer reality, the operational constraint, and the financial guardrail on the table *early*. Backing: team cognition research shows shared understanding improves coordination and performance beyond motivation alone; this is why operating rooms run time-outs and military units brief intent.

---

## 4. Candor, safety, and trust — together

**Present:** People can raise concerns, ask for help, admit uncertainty, and challenge assumptions without social punishment — *and* the purpose is better performance, not emotional insulation. Psychological safety and accountability coexist.

**Absent (symptoms):** One of two pathologies. **Faux harmony** — everyone is "nice," nobody says the hard thing, problems surface late. Or **combative grandstanding** — debate becomes about winning and ego, not the work. (Note: "psychological safety without accountability becomes comfort; accountability without safety becomes silence.")

**Leader's move:** Reward clear dissent. Respond constructively to bad news *every time* — your reaction is the single biggest lever on whether people bring you problems early. Hold equally high standards for preparation and follow-through. Critically: psychological safety is **not** niceness — it is the ability to take interpersonal risks. The best teams are candid, respectful, and demanding. Backing: Edmondson's work (and her explicit rejection of the "safety = nice" reading), the intrateam-trust meta-analysis, and Google's Project Aristotle.

---

## 5. Communication quality over volume

**Present:** The team communicates *precisely*, not just frequently. High-stakes coordination moments are standardized.

**Absent (symptoms):** Message volume substitutes for understanding — more meetings, more Slack, more email, less clarity. Critical commitments are assumed rather than confirmed. "I thought everyone understood" is treated as a communication strategy.

**Leader's move:** Standardize the interaction for recurring high-risk moments: agendas with desired decisions sent in advance, every item labeled update/discussion/decision, the owner opening with a recommendation (not a data dump), read-backs for critical commitments, and written records of key decisions. Backing: communication *quality* is more strongly related to performance than *frequency*; information elaboration and knowledge sharing beat sheer volume. This is the logic behind aviation's standard phraseology, TeamSTEPPS closed-loop communication, and handbook-first remote models.

---

## 6. Learning velocity

**Present:** The team reviews reality faster than weaker teams. Retrospectives are unavoidable after launches, crises, and major meetings, and every lesson carries an owner and a date.

**Absent (symptoms):** Review is skipped entirely, or it degenerates into blame theater. The same mistakes recur because nothing was carried forward.

**Leader's move:** Make retrospectives structurally unavoidable, and require owners + dates on improvement actions (otherwise it's performative reflection). Use the five-question retro: *what were we trying to achieve / what actually happened / what helped / what hurt / what will we change, by whom, by when.* Backing: this is the shared logic of Army after-action reviews, Google blameless postmortems, and healthcare debriefing — examine the event, understand why, assign changes, carry lessons forward. **Blameless** is the key qualifier: focus on contributing causes, assume people acted with good intent and the information they had.

---

## 7. High standards and mutual accountability

**Present:** Standards are visible and *peers* participate in upholding them. The team is demanding, not merely a supportive community.

**Absent (symptoms):** Accountability is treated as something only the boss can do — which breeds dependency and passive aggression. Missed commitments slide quietly. Resentment accumulates.

**Leader's move:** Publish commitments, review them openly, and make missed commitments *discussable without humiliation but impossible to ignore.* Handle underperformance directly and early — elite teams don't let resentment accumulate. Backing: sports-cohesion research, senior-team research, and high-reliability domains converge here; trust and safety make high standards *easier* to sustain because people confront problems sooner.

---

## Using this in conversation

- Reflect the user's situation back as a condition: *"This sounds less like a motivation problem and more like #2 — nobody actually owns the decision."*
- It's common for two conditions to interlock (e.g. weak #4 hides weak #6 — no safety, so no honest retro). Name the chain.
- Then route to the fix: `operating-system.md` for structural installs, `leadership-moves.md` for in-the-moment behavior, `diagnostic.md` to score the whole team.
