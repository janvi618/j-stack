# The Evidence Base

Use this when a user wants to know *what the research actually supports* — to cite evidence, to calibrate how confident to be, or to avoid over-claiming. The honest framing: the evidence is **strongest for broad team conditions** (clarity, trust, shared mental models, communication quality, training, review loops) and **weaker for fashionable tools and anecdotes.** Don't oversell. Tiered below from well-supported → context-dependent → weak.

---

## Well-supported findings

**Team process factors.** Effectiveness scholarship consistently points to a small set of recurring factors: leadership, mutual monitoring, backup behavior, adaptability, and team orientation — supported by shared mental models, closed-loop communication, and mutual trust. (Plain English: top teams know what matters, know who owns what, notice drift quickly, help each other without drama, and update faster.)

**Psychological safety.** Edmondson defined it as a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking; later meta-analytic work found it positively related to learning behavior, performance, and citizenship behavior. Real, but not magic — and explicitly *not* the same as niceness.

**Trust.** A major meta-analysis found intrateam trust is positively related to team performance and remains important even after controlling for other variables. Matters even more in virtual teams; documentation can partially complement trust by reducing ambiguity.

**Role clarity & shared mental models.** Among the best predictors of coordinated performance. Team-cognition research shows shared understanding of task, environment, and who-knows-what improves coordination and performance *beyond motivation alone.*

**Communication quality > quantity.** Not all information sharing is equal — unique information and high-quality exchange predict performance better than talking more. A meta-analysis found communication *quality* is more strongly related to performance than *frequency*, and that information elaboration and knowledge sharing outperform sheer volume.

**Goals.** A meta-analysis on group goal-setting found *specific, difficult goals* improve group performance versus vague goals.

**Training.** A major meta-analysis found team training is a viable, meaningful way to improve team outcomes. (This is why elite domains invest so heavily in simulation, rehearsal, briefing, and review — high performance is *trained coordination*, not natural chemistry.)

**Conflict (nuanced).** The broad result: *both* relationship conflict and task conflict are, on average, negatively associated with performance and satisfaction. But task conflict can become useful under conditions of psychological safety, and in top management teams trust helps keep task disagreement from turning into personal hostility. Correct lesson: not "encourage conflict" but *create conditions where rigorous disagreement stays about the work.*

**Leadership (mostly via conditions).** Leadership behaviors explain meaningful variance in team learning — person-focused leadership especially helps developmental learning, task-focused especially helps adaptive learning. Leadership matters mostly *through the conditions it creates.*

**Behavioral integration (senior teams).** Executive teams perform better when they actually exchange information, collaborate, and decide *jointly* — rather than meeting as a collection of functional heads.

## Promising but context-dependent

**Shared leadership.** Meta-analyses generally find a positive relationship with team effectiveness — leadership functions *can* be distributed beneficially. The catch: it still requires clear architecture — someone must define decision rules, integrate viewpoints, and resolve deadlocks. High-performing teams are *less* leaderless than the literature implies; they're better at distributing the *right* leadership behavior at the right moment. **Shared leadership is not a substitute for accountability.**

**Team charters.** Useful, but the evidence is more modest and context-specific than workshop facilitators imply — strongest in student and project-team settings, especially when charters are *revisited* rather than treated as one-time. In the workplace, a charter helps only if linked to real trade-offs, decision rights, and review routines. *A laminated values poster is not a charter.*

**Diversity.** Real but not automatic — meta-analytic work shows *mixed* direct effects on performance. Diversity improves outcomes when the team is equipped to surface, integrate, and use differentiated expertise; otherwise it can increase misunderstanding or partitioning. Correct lesson: *different expertise produces value only when communication and integration are strong.*

## Popular but weak or incomplete

**"Hire only A players."** One of the weakest popular ideas here. Collective-intelligence research found group performance was *not* predicted by the maximum or average intelligence of members — social sensitivity and balanced conversational participation mattered more. If one or two stars dominate airtime, the team can become dumber than its résumé suggests.

**"Psychological safety means being nice."** Wrong. Edmondson explicitly argued against the "low standards / anything goes" reading. Best interpretation: *candid, respectful, demanding.* Remove fear *and* preserve standards → learning. Remove standards for comfort → drift.

## How to use this honestly

- When citing, distinguish the tiers — "this is well-established" vs "this is promising but depends on context" vs "this is popular but the evidence is thin." Credibility comes from *not* overclaiming.
- The strongest, safest claims to stand behind: clear goals, trust, shared mental models, communication quality, training, and review loops. Lean on these.
- Flag the limits when relevant: effects of specific fashionable tools and single-company anecdotes are more context-dependent than the strongest claims suggest, and cross-domain translation always requires judgment.
- Source documents (NTSB, FAA, WHO, AHRQ, Army doctrine, CAIB, Google SRE, and the underlying meta-analyses) are listed in the original playbook if a user wants to go to primary sources.
