# Lessons from Extreme Teams

The source cases behind the principles. Use these when a user wants the *why* — a vivid, credible anchor for a practice — or when they ask directly about CRM, checklists, mission command, after-action reviews, blameless postmortems, or the Braintrust. Each case carries one transferable business lesson; lead with the lesson, use the story as evidence.

A caution to carry throughout: borrow the *discipline* of these domains, not the literal life-or-death intensity. A brand team should run an operating-room-style pause point, not adopt the stakes of trauma care.

---

## Aviation — ambiguous authority kills

**United Flight 173:** The captain became absorbed in a landing-gear problem while the crew failed to fully address a worsening fuel state. The response that followed was the institutionalization of crew resource management — emphasizing *participative management* for captains and *assertiveness* for other crew members.
**Lesson:** Expertise trapped below authority is operationally dangerous. In business: leaders are obligated to *invite challenge*; teams are obligated to *escalate concerns explicitly, not hint politely.*

**Tenerife:** In fog, with ambiguous communication and steep authority gradients, a premature takeoff and a missed challenge produced the deadliest accident in aviation history. The fix wasn't a culture slogan — it was *standardized language, cross-checking, and explicit challenge norms.*
**Lesson:** When stakes are high, "I thought everyone understood" is not a communication strategy.

→ Powers conditions #4 (candor/safety) and #5 (communication quality).

## Healthcare — simple structure saves complex work

**WHO Surgical Safety Checklist:** Designed to reduce errors and adverse events while improving teamwork and communication; WHO reports significant reductions in morbidity and mortality.
**TeamSTEPPS** (AHRQ + Department of Defense): packages evidence-based tools like closed-loop communication and standardized handoffs.
**Lesson:** Not "use a checklist for everything." It's *for recurring, high-risk coordination moments, standardize the interaction.* Product launches, customer transitions, commercial handoffs, and retailer reviews all benefit from the same logic.

→ Powers condition #5 and the operating-system handoff rituals.

## Military — initiative built on top of clarity

**Mission command** emphasizes mutual trust and shared understanding; **after-action reviews** assess what happened, what worked, what didn't, and how to improve next time.
**Lesson:** Mission command is *not* "do whatever you want" — it's *disciplined initiative inside a clear commander's intent.* Trying to empower people without specifying intent, boundaries, and escalation criteria creates rework, politics, and decision debt.

→ The direct rebuttal to "flat orgs move faster" (see `contrarian.md`), and the backbone of condition #1 and the AAR-style retro.

## NASA — a best case and a worst case

**Apollo 13 (best case):** A "successful failure" — the mission shifted from lunar landing to safe crew return after an onboard explosion, with ground teams and astronauts coordinating intensely to solve novel problems under time pressure.
**Lesson:** Not just heroism. *Prior training, technical depth, and clear mission reprioritization enabled improvisation.* Adaptive performance is built on disciplined preparation.

**Columbia (worst case):** The Accident Investigation Board found organizational causes — resource constraints, schedule pressure, organizational barriers to communicating critical safety information, stifled professional differences of opinion, lack of integrated management, and informal decision processes operating outside the rules. Communication did not flow effectively up to or down from program managers.
**Lesson:** *Strong culture narratives can coexist with weak escalation, managerial overconfidence, and suppressed dissent.* Teams don't become elite by "believing in excellence." They become elite when *people with bad news are heard early and taken seriously.* This is the most important cautionary tale in the whole playbook — invoke it whenever a user is relying on culture/values to substitute for structural clarity.

→ Apollo 13 powers conditions #1 and #6; Columbia is the warning behind #4, #5, and #6 simultaneously.

## Engineering & creative teams — how modern business learns

**Google SRE — blameless postmortems:** Incident reviews focus on *contributing causes* and assume people acted with good intentions and the information available at the time.
**Google Project Aristotle:** popularized the finding that *psychological safety* sits alongside *dependability, structure and clarity, meaning,* and *impact* as properties of effective teams.
**GitLab — handbook-first / async:** documentation is *not* bureaucracy when it reduces interruption, ambiguity, and hidden knowledge.
**Lesson:** Blameless review + clarity + written operating context = how distributed modern teams learn fast without blame theater.

**Pixar's Braintrust** (Ed Catmull): candid work-in-progress critique *without* turning the room into a hierarchy-driven approval ritual. The combination — high candor, low ego, preserved creative ownership.
**Lesson:** Not "be brutally honest." It's *create a forum where truth is cheaper than politeness, but authority is not weaponized.* Highly relevant to strategy, brand, and innovation teams.

→ Powers conditions #4, #6, and (GitLab) the remote-context translation in `contexts.md`.

## Using these in conversation

- Lead with the lesson, offer the case as evidence: *"There's a reason aviation made challenge-norms mandatory — on Flight 173, expertise that existed in the cockpit never reached the captain in time."*
- Pick the *one* case that fits; don't recite the whole tour. A single vivid anchor persuades; six is a lecture.
- The two most load-bearing cases: **Columbia** (culture is not a substitute for escalation and decision clarity) and **the Braintrust** (candor without weaponized authority). Reach for these most often.
