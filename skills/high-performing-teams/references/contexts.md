# Applying the Lessons by Context

The same principles, translated for the team type the user actually leads. Match the user's situation, then give the translation — not the generic theory.

---

## Executive / leadership teams

**The real failure mode:** Not low intelligence — *low behavioral integration.* The functions are present, but the team doesn't actually think together. (Behavioral integration = genuinely exchanging information, collaborating, and making decisions jointly, rather than meeting as a collection of functional heads.) The Columbia warning applies in miniature: conflicting goals, schedule pressure, and fragmented authority make senior teams dangerously overconfident.

**The translation:**
- Fewer status meetings, more **joint problem-solving.**
- Fewer vague strategic themes, more **explicit enterprise trade-offs.**
- Fewer bilateral side deals, more **transparent decisions in the room.**

If the user runs an exec team that "meets a lot but doesn't decide," this is the diagnosis. The side-deal pattern in particular — where real alignment happens in 1:1s and the team meeting is theater — is the thing to break.

---

## Cross-functional project, product, strategy, and brand teams

**The real failure mode:** These teams need *shared mental models*, not inspirational slogans. When work is interdependent, the team can look collaborative while still failing because the functions never integrated their views into one plan.

**The translation:** Align explicitly on **user/consumer problem, success metric, timeline, risk profile, and decision owner.** Design the team to *surface what only one function knows* — unique information is the most valuable kind, and it's exactly what gets discovered too late. Get the consumer insight, the customer/retailer reality, the manufacturing constraint, and the financial guardrail on the table *early*, not sequentially in late-stage conflict.

---

## Sales teams and sales–marketing–customer interfaces

**The real failure mode:** Confusing speed with heroics. If marketing builds material the field doesn't use, or customer teams commit what supply chain can't deliver, the failure isn't effort — it's *coordination.*

**The translation (borrow from aviation and healthcare — strong handoffs, explicit contingency planning, visible ownership):**
- Standardized **pre-call briefs** and **post-call debriefs.**
- **Retailer-issue escalation rules.**
- **Closed-loop confirmation** of customer commitments.

Treat marketing↔sales as an **operating interface, not a relationship favor.** Marketing delivers agreed assets, claims, launch timing, and communication windows tied to specific commercial moments — not vague "support." Sales feeds back customer objections, retailer constraints, and field learnings *in a standardized format* — not vague "keeping marketing informed."

---

## Operations teams and hybrid/remote teams

**The real failure mode:** Trust still matters (it matters *more* in virtual teams), but the operating model has to change. The instinct is to add meetings; the correct move is to add *explicitness.*

**The translation:** Remote teams require **more explicitness, not more meetings.** Document decisions, owners, status, and assumptions *by default* (handbook-first). Reserve meetings for **ambiguity reduction, conflict resolution, or genuine synthesis** — never for broadcasting information that could have been written. Written, searchable operating context is what reduces friction when you can't read the room.

---

## CPG-specific application

In CPG, **many of the hardest team problems are coordination problems disguised as people problems.** A brand team can look collaborative while failing because consumer insight, retailer reality, supply constraints, and margin guardrails were never integrated into one shared plan.

**The core fix — one shared mental model:** *one demand-side thesis, one business case, one operations reality, one decision owner.*

**Customer teams & retailer partnerships:** The highest-value practice is **structured pre-brief and debrief discipline.** Before a retailer meeting, align on objective, fallback positions, non-negotiables, and escalation thresholds. After, confirm commitments, open questions, and owners *in writing.* It's simple because it is simple — the power is consistency.

**Sales–marketing collaboration:** Improves when the handoff is *explicit* (see the sales section above). Treat it as an operating interface.

**Innovation launches & commercialization** (especially vulnerable to late-stage conflict): Use an **operating-room mindset — stage the critical pause points.** Before commercialization, force a *joint* review of: consumer proposition, pack and claims, production feasibility, forecast integrity, customer sell-in timing, and margin risk. Require **one decision-maker and one escalation path.** If speed matters, **narrow the number of true approvers** rather than asking everyone to "align."

**Supply chain × finance trade-offs** expose whether a CPG team is *actually* a team. When service risk, margin erosion, and customer pressure collide, elite teams don't disappear into functions — they **huddle, restate the primary objective, surface the real trade-off, and make the call with visible ownership.** The worst version is performative collaboration: endless discussion, no decision-maker, blame distributed after the fact.

---

## How to deploy

- Lead with the *failure mode* for the user's context, then the translation — it lands harder than listing practices.
- Most contexts above are really the same two or three conditions (shared mental models, decision rights, communication quality) wearing different clothes. If the user spans contexts, anchor on the condition.
- For CPG specifically, the recurring move is: *coordination problem disguised as a people problem → one shared plan with one decision owner.* Reach for it whenever a CPG user describes interpersonal friction on an interdependent launch.
