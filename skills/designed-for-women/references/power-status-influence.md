# Power, status, influence, and social dominance

**Confidence in this domain: high for the core backlash findings; moderate on how much they vary by race, age, and class.** Women get status — that isn't in question. The question is by what route, and the data keep pointing to the same answer: prestige, expertise, and coalition-built credibility travel better for women than borrowed swagger. The backlash around explicit dominance and around anger is one of the more robust findings in social psychology.

The myth worth dismantling: that power advice is gender-neutral so long as it sounds "professional." Much of it isn't neutral at all — it's a set of male norms that got promoted to "leadership" and forgot where it came from.

---

## The reframes (common advice → more evidence-based version)

**"Be dominant."**
The meta-analytic and recent influence work is consistent: explicit dominance is penalised harder for women, men get more mileage from it, and women tend to gain more — at least at first — through prestige. Telling a woman to "command the room" is often telling her to do the one thing the room will punish.
→ **Reframe:** build influence through **expertise, credibility, coalition, and prestige**, not dominance cosplay. *Confidence: high.*

**"Use anger strategically."**
A man's anger reads as conviction; a woman's reads as instability — and the status hit lands on her (HKS status-conferral work, https://gap.hks.harvard.edu/can-angry-woman-get-ahead-status-conferral-gender-and-expression-emotion-workplace). The one thing that reliably softens it is anchoring the anger to a clear external cause, so it's heard as a response to the situation rather than a feature of her temperament.
→ **Reframe:** if anger is warranted, **tie it explicitly to the external cause** — and recognise the asymmetric penalty is a fact about the audience, not a flaw in her. *Confidence: high.*

**"Executive presence is gender-neutral."**
"Executive presence" is one of those phrases that sounds like a quality and functions like a dress code. Leadership stays culturally masculine-coded (Koenig et al., 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21639606/), and women run into a warmth/competence trade-off that men largely don't (https://www.women-unlimited.com/wp-content/uploads/prejudice_against_women.pdf): warm reads as less competent, competent reads as cold.
→ **Reframe:** ignore the vibe and build **credible, expertise-based authority**; don't sacrifice warmth chasing a persona that was never measured on women anyway. *Confidence: high.*

**"Talk more and you'll have more authority."**
Only if you're allowed to finish. Women are interrupted more in public authority settings, and the interruptions themselves erode perceived authority (congressional-hearing study, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/effect-of-gender-on-interruptions-at-congressional-hearings/DDD33F28A1C8ED9C162E1793D8126243). Volume into a room that talks over you just gives it more to talk over.
→ **Reframe:** pair speaking with **agenda ownership, repetition of the key point, ally amplification, and meeting norms that stop interruption.** *Confidence: high.*

**"Confidence displays work the same for everyone."**
They don't decode the same way. The identical confident display reads as competence in a man and as coldness or pushiness in a woman, depending on context (dominance-backlash + warmth/competence literature).
→ **Reframe:** calibrate to the room; the signal is the same but the translation isn't. *Confidence: moderate.*

**"Attractiveness always helps."**
Not for women applying to male-typed roles, where the "beauty is beastly" effect can actively count against her when looks are irrelevant to the job (https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00224540903365414).
→ **Reframe:** appearance is not a clean lever and sometimes cuts the wrong way; stop treating it as an asset to deploy. *Confidence: moderate.*

**"Same behaviour, same status return."**
Not for the same behaviour in a male-typed role: female leaders get devalued harder than identical male counterparts (role-congruity work). The gap isn't in what she did; it's in who did it.
→ **Reframe:** equal behaviour does not buy equal returns, and that's a fact about the scorers, not her performance. *Confidence: high.*

**"Masculine norms define real authority."**
The mixed organisational and social-psych literature says otherwise: women reach influence more reliably through expertise, credibility, coalition, and prestige than through dominance mimicry. Authority that doesn't trigger backlash is also just more durable.
→ **Reframe:** the prestige route is both more effective and less costly for women — it's the better strategy, not the consolation prize. *Confidence: moderate.*

## Life stage (power-specific)

- **Early adulthood:** self-promotion norms and social comparison run hot, and confidence advice gets tangled with image and safety pressures.
- **Midlife / perimenopause:** appearance and age penalties sharpen, and symptoms read as ordinary stress get quietly held against her competence.
- **Older adulthood:** ageism and visibility penalties climb.

## Intersectional notes (be honest about thin evidence)

- The literature is **genuinely mixed** on how much backlash varies by race, age, and class. Intersectional work says the modifiers matter, but real-organisation studies measuring backlash *and* race *and* age *and* role composition at once are a documented gap — so don't hand over a confident intersectional answer the evidence won't back.
- **Older women, non-white women, and women in blue-collar or public-sector roles** are especially underrepresented here.

## Questions worth handing her

- Does this advice quietly **assume male-coded behaviour is neutral**?
- Were **likability and backlash costs** measured, or only "effectiveness"?
- Is the recommended move **prestige-based** (expertise, coalition) or **dominance-based** (the one that gets punished harder)?
