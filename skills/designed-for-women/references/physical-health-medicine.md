# Physical health and medicine

**Confidence in this domain: HIGH — this is where the male-default problem is real, large, and well-documented, so be confident here.** The short version: for most of modern medical history the default research body was male, and women got treated as small men with inconvenient hormones. Women were broadly excluded from early drug trials (1977 FDA policy), they remain underrepresented relative to disease burden across several specialties, they're frequently *not analysed separately* even when enrolled, and the downstream result is sex-blind dosing, delayed diagnosis, and missing evidence in pregnancy, lactation, endometriosis, and menopause. When a recommendation feels generic, the first question is whether anyone checked it in a female body.

**But conviction is not licence to invent differences, and this is the discipline that separates good physiology from marketing.** The document is explicit, so hold the line: chest pain is still the most common heart-attack symptom in women; basic strength-training principles work for women; rigid menstrual-cycle "syncing" is weakly supported. The strongest sex-specific evidence is in **cardiovascular disease, pain, immune function, sleep, long COVID, and drug response** — not only reproduction. Amplify the real mechanism; don't manufacture one for effect.

**This is the domain to say it straight: if a symptom is being minimised, it is worth proper medical attention, and a second opinion is reasonable.** Never diagnose. Do arm her with the specific question and push her to escalate when something's being waved off. The voice here is practical and physiological — explain the *why* in the body — but it stops at the clinic door for anything diagnostic or pharmacological: there the job is the right question, not the verdict.

## The reframes (common advice → more evidence-based version)

**"Standard medical evidence applies equally to women."**
It often hasn't been tested that way. Women remain underrepresented relative to disease burden in cardiology, oncology, neurology, immunology, and hematology (Steinberg et al., 2021, analysis of 20,020 U.S. trials, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24773399/), and enrolment isn't the same as analysis — acute-care trials routinely fail to break results out by sex (Granton et al., 2024, *BMJ Open*). "Included" and "studied" are different words.
→ **Reframe:** ask whether the evidence behind a recommendation is **sex-disaggregated**, and whether women were enrolled in proportion to who actually gets the disease. *Confidence: high.*

**"Just take the standard dose."**
Sometimes fine; sometimes not, and the difference is pharmacokinetic, not trivial. Women clear **zolpidem** more slowly, so the same bedtime dose leaves more drug on board in the morning — which is why the FDA cut the recommended dose for women (https://www.fda.gov/files/drugs/published/Drug-Safety-Communication--Risk-of-next-morning-impairment-after-use-of-insomnia-drugs--FDA-requires-lower-recommended-doses-for-certain-drugs-containing-zolpidem-%28Ambien--Ambien-CR--Edluar--and-Zolpimist%29.pdf). That's one drug where the difference was caught; the worry is the ones nobody checked.
→ **Reframe:** ask whether **sex-disaggregated dosing and pregnancy/lactation/life-stage data exist** for this specific medication. *Confidence: high — but this is "ask the question," not "halve your dose yourself." Dosing changes go through the prescriber.*

**"Heart attacks in women are mostly atypical." → this one is a dangerous overcorrection; correct it head-on.**
Chest pain or discomfort is still the **most common** heart-attack symptom in women (AHA, https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/heart-attack/warning-signs-of-a-heart-attack/heart-attack-symptoms-in-women). Women *more often also* get jaw/neck/back pain, nausea, shortness of breath, and fatigue, and carry female-specific risk enhancers across the life course. The fashionable "women don't get chest pain, they get subtle symptoms" line is wrong, and it has caused women to sit at home through an MI. Both/and, not either/or.
→ **Reframe:** the extra symptoms are **additional, not a substitute.** Exertional chest symptoms get evaluated promptly, full stop. *Confidence: high. This is a place the skill must NOT invent a difference.*

**"Pain is assessed and treated objectively."**
The pain literature frequently doesn't disaggregate by sex, and women's pain is more likely to be undertreated or reframed as psychological (IASP, https://www.iasp-pain.org/resources/fact-sheets/sex-gender-biases-in-pain-research-and-clinical-practice/). "It's probably anxiety" is doing a lot of unearned work in a lot of exam rooms.
→ **Reframe:** if pain is being minimised or written off as stress without a workup, **documenting it and asking for a second opinion is reasonable and often necessary.** *Confidence: high. Encourage escalation.*

**"Cycle-syncing your workouts is evidence-based." → borrow the conviction, decline the claim.**
This is where the "women are not small men" energy gets overstretched in the wild, so be precise: current systematic reviews do **not** show menstrual-cycle-phase effects on strength adaptation large enough to justify rigid phase-based programming (https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4663/12/1/31). The honest physiological position is that **basic strength-training principles work for women** — progressive overload, adequate protein, recovery — and that tailoring to *how she actually feels and performs* beats syncing to a calendar.
→ **Reframe:** train hard and progressively; **calibrate fuelling and recovery to symptoms, performance, health status, and life stage**, not to a cycle-phase chart. The big lever is usually under-fuelling, not mis-timing. *Confidence: moderate-to-high against rigid syncing.*

**"Excluding pregnant and breastfeeding women protects them."**
It protects the trial, not the patient. Exclusion leaves gaping holes in dosing, efficacy, and safety, and then clinicians have to make decisions in the dark anyway (WHO 2025, https://www.who.int/news/item/18-06-2025-equity-and-health--the-inclusion-of-pregnant-and-breastfeeding-women-in-clinical-trials; National Academies, https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/urgent-action-needed-from-nih-fda-congress-and-hhs-to-improve-inclusion-of-pregnant-and-lactating-women-in-clinical-trials-says-new-report).
→ **Reframe:** "no data" does **not** mean "safe" — it means nobody measured. In pregnancy and lactation, push for an explicit risk/benefit conversation rather than a shrug. *Confidence: high.*

**"Female-specific conditions are well covered."**
They are not. Endometriosis takes *years* to diagnose; PCOS is common and finally has a strong 2023 international guideline, but implementation is patchy. Women routinely get told period pain this severe is normal. It isn't.
→ **Reframe:** long diagnostic delay is the norm here, not bad luck — persistence and second opinions are warranted, especially for endometriosis-pattern symptoms. *Confidence: high.*

**"Sex differences only matter for reproduction."**
The lazy assumption, and false. Women are disproportionately hit by autoimmune disease, have higher insomnia prevalence, and carry higher long-COVID risk (modified by age, pregnancy, menopause). The female body is not a male body with a reproductive module bolted on; the differences run through immune, sleep, cardiovascular, and post-viral systems.
→ **Reframe:** sex-specific health is **system-wide**, not confined to the pelvis. *Confidence: high.*

## Life stage (health-specific) — the part generic advice ignores

- **Pregnancy:** major cardiovascular, immune, metabolic, and pharmacokinetic shifts; many "general" recommendations get *less* evidence-based here precisely because pregnant women were excluded from the trials.
- **Postpartum:** recovery, broken sleep, lactation, hormonal cliff — generic training and productivity advice is badly calibrated, and "bounce back" framing is actively unhelpful.
- **Perimenopause:** sleep disruption, vasomotor symptoms, shifting estrogen, higher depression risk — and a strong tendency for all of it to be filed as ordinary stress.
- **Menopause:** cardiovascular risk profile changes and hormone-therapy evidence becomes relevant; any health advice that stops at fertility is failing her here.
- **Older adulthood:** chronic disease burden, pain, and changing social networks all weigh more.

## Intersectional notes (be honest about thin evidence)

- **Women of colour** are underrepresented in clinical research, with real bias in access and assessment on top.
- **Trans and gender-diverse people** are underrepresented in drug and biologic trials despite greater need, and many "women's" studies exclude or misclassify them.
- **Disabled women** face compounded safety, pain, and access barriers, but cross-domain synthesis is sparse — say so rather than improvising.

## Questions worth handing her

- Were women **analysed separately**, or just enrolled and averaged in?
- Were **pregnant / postpartum / perimenopausal** women included in whatever this rests on?
- Is this **symptom profile actually validated in women**?
- Does **sex-disaggregated dosing** exist for this drug — and has anyone checked it against my life stage?
- (When something's being minimised:) is a **second opinion** reasonable here? Usually, yes.
