# Methods, Cases, and Practice

Supporting material for going beyond the core coaching loop: how first principles relates to adjacent methods, real cases that show its value *and* its limits, common business reframings, a practice plan, and a reading path.

## How it sequences with adjacent methods

First principles is not a competing "school" that replaces other methods — it's a reset mode. The practical rule: **use first principles when you suspect the frame is wrong; use adjacent methods to enrich the work once the frame is reset.** In a corporate setting the best sequence is often: **first principles → systems / JTBD / MECE → hypothesis testing → experiment or pilot.**

| Method | What it is | Key difference from first principles | When to use it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Systems thinking** | Feedback loops, interdependence, emergence | Focuses on relationships and dynamics, not just fundamentals | Complex cross-functional or market systems |
| **Design thinking** | Human-centered, iterative; empathy + prototyping | Starts from lived user experience more than abstract fundamentals | Consumer-facing innovation, service redesign |
| **Root cause analysis** | Structured investigation of failures | Usually retrospective and problem-specific | Quality failures, incidents, defects |
| **Five Whys** | Repeatedly asking "why" beneath symptoms | Simpler and more linear than robust first-principles work | Quick diagnosis of process failures |
| **MECE decomposition** | Non-overlapping, exhaustive categories | Organizes; doesn't by itself identify what's fundamental | Strategy problem structuring |
| **Hypothesis-driven consulting** | Form high-value hypotheses early, test fast | Starts from likely explanations, not necessarily foundations | Large ambiguous questions under time pressure |
| **Scientific method** | Build testable hypotheses, evaluate vs evidence | Stricter about testing and falsification | Any decision where experiments are possible |
| **Socratic questioning** | Disciplined questioning to expose contradictions | More dialogic/interrogative than solution-rebuild oriented | Meetings, coaching, strategy debate |
| **Inversion** | Think backwards: how to fail / what to avoid | Flips the problem rather than reducing it to fundamentals | Risk management, decision hygiene |
| **Jobs-to-Be-Done** | The progress a customer is trying to make in context | Centers customer causality, not broad system fundamentals | Innovation, portfolio strategy, segmentation |
| **Lean startup** | Build-measure-learn via MVPs | An execution/experimentation system | New ventures, pilots, innovation bets |
| **Blue ocean strategy** | Reconstruct market boundaries, uncontested space | A strategic-market method, not a reasoning discipline | Category creation, repositioning |
| **Bayesian reasoning** | Update beliefs as evidence arrives | About belief updating under uncertainty | Forecasting, diagnosis, dynamic decisions |
| **Critical thinking** | Evaluate claims, evidence, arguments | Broader and more general than first principles | Everyday managerial judgment |
| **Lateral thinking** | Generate non-obvious ideas and connections | Emphasizes novelty over foundations | Ideation stalls, constrained brainstorming |
| **Abductive reasoning** | Inference to the best explanation | Generates plausible explanations; doesn't prove fundamentals | Diagnosing ambiguous market shifts |
| **Scenario planning** | Explore multiple plausible futures | Focuses on external uncertainty, not root structure alone | Long-range strategy, disruptive environments |

## Real-world cases — value *and* limits

The point of every case below is the same: first principles plus execution, never first principles instead of it.

- **Toyota** — Disciplined depth, not heroic genius. The Toyota Production System pursues waste elimination and repeatedly asks "why" to reach underlying causes. *Limit:* it works because it's embedded in process discipline, not because of one clever contrarian insight.
- **Amazon** — The core move is "fundamental user need," not blank-slate ontology. "Working backwards" from the customer (via PR/FAQ-style docs) refuses proxy metrics. *Lesson:* the fastest way to challenge stale assumptions is often to ask what the customer is actually trying to achieve, before channels/tech/silos.
- **IKEA** — Clean first-principles logic on a mass market: well-designed functional products at prices so low most people can afford them. Flat-pack and self-serve warehouse logic *follow from* that. The challenged assumption: that furniture value requires traditional store service, bulky transport, and pre-assembly. *Limit:* it works only because customers accept assembly and standardization — coherent, not universally superior.
- **Costco / Trader Joe's** — Both challenge the assumption that grocers maximize value by carrying *more* assortment. Costco: low prices on a limited selection plus a "treasure hunt" feel. Trader Joe's: small stores, limited freezer space, tasting, a kaizen ethos. The principle: simplicity can produce better economics and clearer choice. *Limit:* curation has to be genuinely strong, and less assortment creates its own exclusions.
- **SpaceX** — The most mythologized case. The *documented* part: reusability as the mechanism for lowering launch cost, with milestone first-stage landings. The *oversimplified* part: the tale that they simply reasoned from raw-material prices. Better reading: engineering fundamentals + manufacturing focus + enormous experimental iteration.
- **Netflix** — Repeated reframing around the user and the system (DVD→streaming, US→global, licensed→originals), plus recommendation work starting from "right content, right member, right time." *Lesson:* first-principles reframing is often strategic, not merely technical. *Limit:* these bets were capital-intensive and timing-dependent — not derivable from thought alone.
- **Oral rehydration therapy** — The public-health example worth knowing: a simple, cheap, life-saving glucose-electrolyte solution grounded in what the body physiologically needs. It looks almost trivial in hindsight — which is the point. Returning to fundamentals sometimes beats high-tech alternatives precisely because it scales.

## Common business reframings

A menu of conventional framings and their first-principles counterparts — useful for recognizing when someone is optimizing inside a stale frame.

| Use case | Conventional framing | First-principles reframing | Main tradeoff |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brand strategy | "We need another campaign." | "What drives preference, availability, and repeat in this category?" | Shifts spend away from familiar brand-building rituals |
| Product innovation | "We need more ideas in the funnel." | "What progress is underserved, and what must a better solution do?" | Requires stronger kill discipline |
| Category growth | "Win more shelf." | "What makes the whole category worth buying more often?" | Growth may help competitors too |
| Pricing & promotion | "Discount harder to regain volume." | "What value exchange actually changes shopper behavior profitably?" | Short-term volume risk |
| Retail strategy | "Fix relationships account by account." | "What do retailers fundamentally reward?" | May reduce SKU count |
| Consumer insights | "Run another attitudes study." | "What causes purchase in the moment?" | Slower than relying on old tracking |
| AI adoption | "Roll out tools and train everyone." | "Which jobs create value, and where does friction overwhelm?" | Smaller initial scope |
| Productivity | "We need better time management." | "What activities create output versus activity theater?" | Some leaders may feel loss of visibility |
| Supply chain | "Forecast better." | "What causes variability, and which buffers matter most?" | Requires cross-functional cooperation |
| Org design | "Reorg the chart." | "What decisions need to be made faster, by whom, with what info?" | Less glamorous than a formal reorg |
| Meeting culture | "People need to be more concise." | "What work belongs in meetings at all?" | Forces habit change |
| Budgeting | "Defend line items." | "What resources most directly create the outcomes we say matter?" | Politically sensitive |
| Career strategy | "Get the next title." | "What capabilities and reputational assets compound over time?" | May delay cosmetic wins |

## A 30-day practice plan

Short enough to survive a real job, specific enough to create repetition.

| Days | Theme | Daily exercise |
|---|---|---|
| 1–7 | See assumptions | 10 min/day rewriting one problem into facts vs assumptions; end with one "what must be true?" |
| 8–14 | Decompose | One problem/day broken into drivers, constraints, feedback loops — on real work |
| 15–21 | Rebuild | One problem/day: generate three options from fundamentals; force one that *removes* complexity |
| 22–28 | Test | Turn one assumption/day into a cheap test: question, pilot, analysis, memo, behavior change, pre-mortem |
| 29–30 | Reflect & standardize | Review which assumption types you miss most; build your personal checklist |

## Staged reading path

| Level | Resource | What it teaches | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beginner | *Thinking in Systems* (Meadows) | Feedback loops, stocks/flows, leverage points | Can make readers over-systematize human problems |
| Beginner | Christensen Institute's Jobs-to-Be-Done materials | Customer causality in context | Can under-weight channel economics |
| Beginner | Amazon's "Working Backwards" explainer | Starting from the customer outcome | Not pure first principles; more customer-outcome design |
| Intermediate | Feynman's "Cargo Cult Science" | Evidence, honesty, why rigor beats theater | More epistemic than managerial |
| Intermediate | SEP entry on the scientific method | Hypotheses, method, objectivity, evidence | Philosophical rather than operational |
| Intermediate | *The Book of Why* (Pearl & Mackenzie) | Correlation vs intervention vs counterfactuals | Conceptually demanding for some |
| Intermediate | Official Lean Startup materials | Build-measure-learn, validated learning | Over-applied where experiments are hard |
| Advanced | SEP on Aristotle's metaphysics & causality | Causes, principles, explanatory depth | Rich but not business-ready without translation |
| Advanced | SEP/IEP on Descartes's method | Foundational skepticism, rebuilding belief | Too radical if copied literally |
| Advanced | Price et al. on expert problem solving; Peerally et al. on RCA limits | What experts actually do; why linear diagnoses fail | More analytic than inspirational |

## The intellectual lineage (brief)

First principles is not one thing. In **philosophy** it's about the foundations of knowledge (Aristotle's inquiry into first causes; Descartes's methodic doubt — "demolish everything completely and start again from the foundations"). In **science** it's causal explanation and testability (the scientific method; Feynman's discipline of *not fooling yourself*). In **engineering** it's stable, modelable constraints and rebuilds. In **business** it's a hybrid of customer-need reframing, decomposition, and experimentation. The best modern practice borrows from all four — which is also why the term gets used loosely.

## What the research actually supports

There's no single peer-reviewed field called "first-principles thinking." The support is *indirect but real*: the component skills — decomposition, metacognition, critical thinking, causal reasoning, analogical transfer, experimentation — are well-supported, and they work best **combined with domain knowledge and feedback**. Equally important are the documented *limits*: causal reasoning is hard, debiasing is fragile, and far-transfer from generic "think better" training is often weak. The honest takeaway: practice first principles on the *actual classes of problems you face* (pricing, category growth, AI adoption, meetings, fitness) rather than as an abstract habit — and treat it as a high-value discipline for reframing specific hard problems, not a universal substitute for expertise.
