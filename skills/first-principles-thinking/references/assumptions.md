# Assumption Surfacing

Assumption analysis is where most of the leverage in first-principles work sits. The single most useful habit: whenever a problem feels stuck, inventory the assumptions across *multiple* categories, not just the obvious ones. Most bad strategy hides in assumptions, not spreadsheets.

## The assumption typology

Walk these categories when helping someone go wide. For each, the diagnostic question is the fastest way to pull the hidden belief into the open. Examples below lean CPG/corporate but the categories are general.

| Assumption type | What it looks like | Example | Diagnostic question |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Explicit** | Stated openly in decks and plans | "The category is price elastic." | What evidence supports this right now? |
| **Implicit** | Unstated but embedded in decisions | Everyone assumes premium shoppers will never trade down | What are we behaving as if is true? |
| **Industry** | "This is how the category works." | Innovation must come through flavor extensions | Which category rule is actually just habit? |
| **Organizational** | Internal dogma | Brand owns growth; sales only executes | What internal boundary are we treating as natural? |
| **Customer** | Stories about what buyers value | Shoppers want more choice, not less | What job is the customer actually hiring this product to do? |
| **Economic** | Margin, elasticity, and cost beliefs | We can't raise price without volume collapse | What would have to be true for that belief to fail? |
| **Technical** | What's claimed feasible or infeasible | AI integration requires a full-enterprise platform first | Is this truly impossible, or just inconvenient today? |
| **Cultural** | Norms treated as constraints | Senior leaders expect long meetings to signal seriousness | Which norm is performative rather than necessary? |
| **Identity** | Ego and role beliefs | "We are a premium brand, so we can't simplify." | Which belief protects identity more than results? |
| **Incentive** | What people are rewarded to defend | Trade teams favor volume even if promo economics are poor | Who benefits if we never question this? |
| **Language** | Hidden inside the problem framing | "How do we win shelf back?" assumes shelf is the battleground | What changes if we rewrite the question? |

The last two are easy to miss and unusually high-leverage. **Incentive** assumptions explain why a "wrong" belief survives — someone is rewarded for defending it. **Language** assumptions are smuggled in by the way the question is phrased; re-writing the question often dissolves the problem.

After inventorying, force a prioritization: **which three assumptions are most dangerous?** Going wide is only useful if it converges on the few beliefs that, if false, break the plan.

## Memorizable surfacing questions

These are short on purpose — worth keeping at hand and dropping into any stuck conversation:

- What are we assuming is true?
- What would have to be true for this plan to work?
- Which constraint are we treating as fixed that may actually be flexible?
- What part of this problem is physics, law, or math, and what part is habit, preference, or convention?
- What would an outsider question immediately?
- What would a skeptical customer say?
- What belief here is old enough that no one remembers why it exists?
- What would our competitors hope we never challenge?
- If we had to solve this without the standard playbook, what would we do?
- If the opposite of our current belief were true, what would we notice first?

## The ten best questions, distilled

If you only keep ten:
1. What do we actually know?
2. What are we only assuming?
3. What must be true for this to work?
4. Which constraint is truly hard?
5. Which constraint is only inherited?
6. What causes what here?
7. What would an outsider challenge first?
8. If we started from scratch, what would we build differently?
9. What is the smallest test that would teach us something important?
10. Are we being different, or actually better?
