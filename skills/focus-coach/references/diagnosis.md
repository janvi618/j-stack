# Diagnosing the Actual Blocker

When someone says "I can't focus," that's a symptom, not a cause. The whole value of good coaching is identifying *which* root is actually in play, because they look similar from the outside but need completely different fixes. Prescribing the wrong fix for the right symptom is why most focus advice fails.

This file describes the most common roots, how to tell them apart, and what each one implies. Use it to figure out *why* before you decide *what to do*.

## A quick way in

Listen for what the distraction is *doing for them*. Distraction is rarely random — it's usually solving some problem, even a bad one:
- Relieving an uncomfortable feeling → **emotional avoidance**
- Responding to a cue the environment keeps throwing → **environmental interruption / affordances**
- Filling a gap where there's no clear target → **vague priorities**
- Spreading thin across too many open loops → **overcommitment**
- Reaching for stimulation because the tank is empty → **poor recovery / exhaustion**
- Drifting because the task feels pointless or imposed → **low-quality motivation**
- Genuinely struggling to sustain attention even when they want to → **fragmentation / trained short attention**

Most real situations are a blend. Name the dominant one or two.

## The roots

### 1. Emotional avoidance (distraction as relief)
**What it looks like:** They reach for the phone, the inbox, the snack, the "quick check" right at the moment a task gets hard, ambiguous, or anxiety-provoking. The distraction is a maneuver to escape discomfort, not a genuine need. Often pairs with perfectionism — the task feels so high-stakes that starting is threatening.

**How to tell:** Ask what they were doing *the instant before* they got distracted. If the pattern is "right when it got hard / right when I had to make a decision / right when I opened the scary document," it's avoidance. Procrastination on important-but-daunting work is a classic tell.

**What it implies:** Blockers and discipline won't touch this, because the *feeling* is the driver. The fix is to (a) make the threat smaller — break the task into the smallest visible first action, lower the bar for *starting* while keeping a high bar for *revising later*, and (b) help them notice the avoidance as it happens. Perfectionism is best addressed by reducing the cost of a bad first draft, not by promising oneself excellence.

### 2. Environmental interruption and affordances
**What it looks like:** A phone within reach, an inbox visible in the corner, a Slack badge, an open-plan space, constant pings. The environment is continually *inviting* a switch. People in open and interruptive settings self-interrupt more, and one interruption tends to trigger more later.

**How to tell:** They describe a busy, pingy, exposed setting and the interruptions feel externally cued ("every time I get going, someone messages / a notification pops"). The salient detail is the *scene*, not the feeling.

**What it implies:** This is the most fixable root and the one where design wins cleanly. Environments aren't neutral — they afford and inhibit action. Change what the scene is asking them to do: phone out of sight and reach during focus blocks, only task-relevant tabs open, notifications off, separate visual contexts for deep vs. communication work, headphones/"focus mode" signals. Note: the point is that a *reachable, visible* phone cues checking habits — not a mystical "mere presence" effect (see evidence file). Focus on behavior and cues, not phone superstition.

### 3. Vague priorities (no stable target)
**What it looks like:** They sit down "to work" without a concrete outcome, drift between tabs, end the day busy but unsure what they actually accomplished. There's nothing specific for attention to lock onto, so it scatters by default.

**How to tell:** Ask what the *concrete next outcome* was when they sat down. If they can't name a specific deliverable or first move — just a vague area ("work on the project") — that's the issue. Goal ambiguity is a major, underrated cause of poor focus.

**What it implies:** Goal clarity is one of the strongest antecedents of both focus and flow. The fix is upstream of any technique: before starting, write the one concrete outcome and the first visible next step. Five to ten minutes of this at the start of a session does more than any app. "What does the next chunk produce, and what's the very first action?"

### 4. Overcommitment (too many open loops)
**What it looks like:** They're trying to advance many things at once. Attention fragments because selective attention requires *excluding* alternatives, and there are too many live priorities to exclude any of them. Often felt as a low hum of being behind on everything.

**How to tell:** Count the active priorities. If they're juggling many significant goals simultaneously, or every project is "in progress," that's it. They may frame it as a focus problem when it's really a *load* problem.

**What it implies:** No technique fixes structural overload. The intervention is subtractive: do fewer things, reduce parallel priorities, choose 1-3 significant outcomes rather than a dozen ambitions. Ambition inflation is upstream of much attention failure — too many goals, too many open loops, too many identities to maintain. The honest message is that depth *excludes*: focusing seriously means neglecting some inputs, delaying some replies, accepting that not everything can be equally attended.

### 5. Poor recovery and exhaustion
**What it looks like:** Attention is brittle, mind-wandering is constant, they reach for stimulation because there's nothing in the tank. Often tied to bad sleep, no breaks, relentless extraction with no replenishment.

**How to tell:** Ask about sleep, breaks, and downtime. Poor sleep reliably harms vigilant attention and executive control. If they're running on empty — late nights, no breaks between demanding bouts, "downtime" that's just more screens — the focus problem is downstream of depletion.

**What it implies:** Don't chase advanced hacks while the basics are broken. Protect bedtime and morning light *before* anything fancy. Build in short movement/visual breaks between demanding bouts. Treat recovery as part of performance, not optional. A flow/focus system requires replenishment, not constant extraction. Also: passive screen "downtime" often does *not* restore directed attention well, especially when saturated with notifications and algorithmic novelty.

### 6. Low-quality motivation
**What it looks like:** The work feels imposed, meaningless, or beyond their ability, so attention won't stick no matter how hard they push. This isn't laziness — it's the absence of the conditions that make sustained attention feel possible.

**How to tell:** The task feels like *someone else's* (no autonomy), feels hopeless (no sense of competence/can't see how to meet the challenge), or feels disconnected from anything they care about (no relatedness/purpose). When they describe the work with resignation rather than anxiety, suspect this.

**What it implies:** Autonomy, competence, and relatedness drive higher-quality motivation, persistence, and creativity. Where possible, restore some authorship over the task, rightsize the challenge so success feels achievable, or connect the work to its human impact (seeing who it helps strengthens motivation). This is why "just try harder" backfires — it ignores the motivational architecture entirely.

### 7. Fragmentation / trained short attention span
**What it looks like:** They genuinely struggle to sustain attention even when they *want* to — can't read more than a few pages without reaching for the phone, can't sit with one thing. Years of low-friction digital novelty have trained shallow switching over deep re-engagement.

**How to tell:** The wanting is there but the *capacity* feels diminished. They report that they used to be able to read/concentrate and now can't, or that any pause triggers an automatic reach for novelty. This is about a built-up habit pattern, not a momentary feeling or a single bad environment.

**What it implies:** The reassuring truth is that attention span is *trainable* — this is recoverable, not a permanent loss. Rebuild gradually: reduce switching, design the environment, use short mindfulness training to catch mind-wandering and return earlier, and treat things like long-form reading as deliberate attention practice that gets easier with reps. Tolerating boredom is itself a skill — boredom is a functional signal of mismatch, and the modern problem is that it gets resolved too fast by digital novelty instead of deeper re-engagement. Sitting with it briefly rebuilds the capacity.

## Putting it together

Don't run this as a checklist out loud. Use it to listen well. Reflect back the root you're hearing ("it sounds like the issue isn't discipline — it's that the moment the work gets hard, checking your phone is an escape hatch"), confirm it lands, and only then move to the matching intervention. Getting the mechanism right is most of the coaching; the fix often follows obviously once the root is named.
