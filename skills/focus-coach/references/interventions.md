# Interventions: Concrete, Role-Scaled Fixes

Once you've named the blocker (see `diagnosis.md`), this file gives realistic, research-backed fixes. The guiding rule: **prescribe the smallest real change, scaled to the person's actual life.** One or two interventions that fit beat a total overhaul that collapses in a week.

A durable focus system does only four things: protects a small number of high-quality attention windows, batches reactive work, increases the odds of entering focused/absorbed states, and preserves recovery so attention doesn't become chronically brittle. Everything below serves one of those four.

## Match the intervention to the root

| If the blocker is... | The highest-leverage fixes are... |
|---|---|
| Emotional avoidance | Break task into smallest visible first action; timed entry ritual; lower bar to *start*, keep high bar to *revise*; notice the avoidance as it happens |
| Environmental interruption | Phone out of reach during blocks; notifications off; only task-relevant tabs; quiet zone / headphones / "focus mode" signal |
| Vague priorities | Write the one concrete outcome + first step *before* starting; weekly pick of 1-3 outcomes |
| Overcommitment | Do fewer things; cut parallel priorities; choose 1-3 significant outcomes, not 12 ambitions |
| Poor recovery | Protect sleep and morning light first; short breaks between bouts; one low-load evening + one longer recovery period weekly |
| Low motivation | Restore some autonomy; rightsize challenge; connect work to its human impact |
| Fragmentation | Reduce switching; short mindfulness reps; rebuild reading/concentration gradually; tolerate boredom |

## Daily practices (the menu — pick a few, don't impose all)

- **Morning setup (5-10 min):** Define one cognitively demanding outcome for the day and its first visible next step. Goal clarity is a top antecedent of both focus and flow.
- **One deep work block (60-90 min):** Protected, ideally early or post-recovery, phone away and messaging off. This is the single most reliable lever against interruption and task-switching cost.
- **Breaks between bouts:** Short movement or visual breaks maintain alertness and slow fatigue accumulation. Not slacking — maintenance.
- **Communication windows:** Check email/chat at set times rather than continuously *when the role allows*. (Heavily role-dependent — see below.)
- **Transition ritual:** Before hard work, clear tabs, write the target, start a timer, breathe for 30-60 seconds. Lowers the friction of entry, which is where avoidance strikes.
- **Shutdown ritual:** List unfinished items, the next step, and tomorrow's top task. Reduces mental residue and makes restarting easier.
- **End-of-day reflection:** Rate attention, energy, and meaning. Builds self-knowledge instead of vague guilt.

## Weekly design

- **Weekly planning:** Choose 1-3 significant outcomes — one strategic, one operational, one personal works well. Not 12. Too many parallel goals destroy selective attention.
- **Calendar design:** Place focus blocks *before* the week fills with meetings. Whatever isn't scheduled gets absorbed by shallow work.
- **Meeting audit:** Monthly, delete / shorten / delegate / async-convert recurring meetings. Meetings are a major source of fragmentation and fatigue.
- **Shallow-work batching:** Group approvals, logistics, expense reports, minor replies into dedicated windows. Preserves longer cognitive windows.
- **Recovery time:** Preserve at least one low-load evening and one longer recovery period weekly. Replenishment, not extraction.
- **Skill block:** One recurring block for deliberate practice in a valued domain. Improving skill raises future challenge-skill fit, which makes flow more likely down the line.

## Environment design (boring on purpose)

The underlying problems are mostly salience, friction, and affordances — not exotic neuroscience, so the fixes are low-tech:
- Phone out of sight *and reach* during hard work. The real mechanism is that a reachable, visible phone cues checking habits — don't oversell a "mere presence" effect.
- Keep only task-relevant tabs open.
- Create separate visual contexts for deep work vs. communication work where possible.
- Use a notebook/scratchpad to externalize next steps so working memory isn't carrying everything at once. (Brief interruptions are destructive partly because they degrade the mental model you're holding — offloading it protects against that.)

## Mental skills

The three most valuable: **tolerating boredom**, **clarifying the next action**, and **noticing when distraction is an emotional maneuver rather than a real need.**
- Short mindfulness training helps people catch mind-wandering and return earlier (small-to-moderate but consistent effect on sustained/executive attention).
- When anxiety masquerades as distraction, break the task into the smallest concrete entry action.
- Address perfectionism by lowering the threshold for *starting* while keeping a high standard for *revising* later — not by demanding excellence up front.

## Communication & social/organizational norms

If they want more focus without seeming unresponsive, the move is to **communicate norms, not silently withdraw:**
- Tell collaborators when you're available, which channels are urgent, and when to expect replies.
- Use shared documents, agendas, and decision memos to cut live back-and-forth.
- Manage upward: leaders often assume constant responsiveness only because nobody proposed an alternative. Frame the case for protected focus in terms of *output quality, risk reduction, and decision quality* — not personal preference.

This matters because email/meetings/open-plan aren't inherently bad — *haphazard, low-friction* versions are what's corrosive. The goal is to reduce unnecessary attentional tax, not to ignore coworkers.

## Scale to the role — this is non-negotiable

Deep-work advice assumes some control over one's calendar. That's realistic for some and unrealistic for others. **Do not prescribe a maker schedule to someone who can't use one.**
- **Individual contributors:** Protect a few non-negotiable blocks for writing/analysis/modeling/problem-solving/reading; batch routine coordination around them.
- **Managers:** The unit of protection is smaller — one or two strategic blocks per week, recovery time after heavy meeting clusters, async pre-reads to compress live discussion, office hours for ad hoc issues. Managers should *not* imitate maker schedules naïvely; they need focus systems that coexist with relational responsibility.
- **Caregivers, teachers, client-service, frontline, founders:** Cannot treat all interruptions as illegitimate. Find the smaller pockets that *are* controllable; don't moralize about the ones that aren't.

A useful heuristic to teach: **classify tasks by attentional demand.** Strategy, writing, analysis, creative synthesis, and hard decisions need high-control attention. Status updates, logistics, approvals, and minor replies don't. Many people fail not from lack of effort but from doing high-attention work in low-attention conditions. The fix is to match task to attention window rather than pretending every hour is cognitively equivalent.

## Remote / hybrid

Don't take a side on "remote vs. office" in the abstract. The evidence points to *matching mode to task*: solitary focus benefits from quiet control (often home); creativity and informal bridging benefit from richer in-person contact. Hybrid works when each mode is aligned with the corresponding kind of work — e.g., heads-down days at home, collaborative days in person.

## Attention beyond work (often the most valuable part)

If the person's whole frame is "be more productive," widen it. Flow and sustained attention are most strongly linked to *meaning and growth*, not just feeling good — so these aren't leftovers after work, they're prime attention practices:
- **Reading:** Long-form reading both *requires* and *trains* sustained attention. If they can't read a few pages without checking the phone, that's a fragmentation symptom, not a taste problem — and it's trainable. Rebuild gradually.
- **Exercise:** Works two ways — improves general executive function/attention, *and* can become its own flow domain when challenge-skill is matched (structured progression, immediate feedback, clear metrics, enough difficulty to demand full engagement). More sustainable than treating it as pure discipline.
- **Relationships:** Presence and real listening require the same exclusion principle as any focus — something else has to be set aside. Fragmented attention makes even close relationships feel thin. Focus extended to people becomes an ethical practice, not just an instrumental one.
- **Leisure:** Distinguish leisure that *deepens* attention (nature, hobbies, crafts, engrossing conversation) from leisure that merely *occupies* it (saturated passive scrolling). The goal isn't banning screens — it's telling restorative leisure from numbing leisure.

## Keep it humane

End every prescription with sustainability in mind. The reliable failure mode is turning a focus system into self-punishment. Aim for "dailyish," not perfect. The point isn't to become rigid, antisocial, or optimized into brittleness — it's to live in recurring contact with activities worthy of full attention.
